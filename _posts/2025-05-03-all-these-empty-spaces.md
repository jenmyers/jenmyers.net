In 1990, I was nine years old and _Twin Peaks_ was on the air. I remember watching it, although I don’t remember how I ended up watching it. I can’t imagine anyone else around me then would have been interested in it, so I must have found my own way to it. Some cosmic gravitation that didn’t make sense to me at the time. At the time, the show didn’t make sense to me either. For years afterward, I would think about _Twin Peaks_ only in glimpses and echoes, as if it were something I had dreamed. Even when I was awake and grown, it seemed as if there was no way to know the whole of what I had once seen and heard. The story existed for me as an empty space that was just as significant, if not more so, than what it had contained.

I kept pace with David Lynch’s work as I got older. When I was sixteen, I was obsessed with _Lost Highway_. I doubled back for _Blue Velvet_ and _Eraserhead_. The millennium turned and _Mulholland Drive_ came out, and I gasped out loud when Rebekah del Rio collapsed in Club Silencio while her voice sang on. I even liked _Inland Empire_, which seemed to shake off a lot of other Lynch devotees. But even though I have been a follower of David Lynch’s work longer and more consistently than I have any other director, it’s almost a surprise to realize it. I hadn’t ever thought about David Lynch’s work as work created by the individual named David Lynch. It was more as if I had absorbed him more as a (even I shudder to use this word) brand, defined by incomprehensibility, mysterious intention and sometimes uncomfortable sincerity. I didn’t begin to think seriously and specifically about Lynch’s work until I was much older and better equipped for the task. When I began to look, what I found were spaces.

There are a lot of empty spaces—spaces empty of people or human actions or consequences—in the town of Twin Peaks. We sit in them, pan through them, are dissolved into them. The hallways of the high school, bright and somber. The interiors of family homes, neat and vacant, a ceiling fan spinning alone. The wind moving through the Douglas firs, with a slight swish of red curtains. Throughout his work, Lynch crafted spaces: _Eraserhead_’s howling industrial wasteland, the liminal dimensions of the open road in _Wild at Heart_ and _Lost Highway_, the sickeningly colored personal prison apartment of _Blue Velvet_’s Dorothy Vallens. Sometimes the emptiness is punctuated by a counterpoint, like a man painstakingly sweeping an after-hours roadhouse floor, or BOB clambering over furniture in an otherwise uninhabited house to get to us. He also devised dedicated witnesses to spaces: the ill-fated observer of the portal at the beginning of _Twin Peaks: The Return_, the Man from Another World sliding through the Red Room, the blue-haired woman in the theater balcony at _Muholland Drive_’s Club Silencio. But we’re all witnesses, aren’t we, looking at the spaces Lynch created, contemplating emptiness.

What does it mean for an audience to contemplate an empty space? Is it still empty if we’re there with it but not inside of it? Maybe it is our distant attention that defines it as empty—because if not for the audience, the witnesses, who would even know the difference? The truth is that no empty space is truly empty once we witness it. We fill empty spaces with ourselves: our memories, our expectations, our worries, our discomfort. We give spaces significance. The empty classroom seat is not just a chair without anyone sitting in it—it’s the instantly recognizable hole that Laura Palmer left in the world. Maybe the converse is also true—maybe we only truly exist when we show up to witness, when we make the effort to notice and pay attention and are rewarded with the unlocked opportunity for significance. The film could run in a dark theater long after the film director is gone, but it’s not complete without the audience, seated and watching, bright eyes and clear faces only illuminated by the light from the screen.

In January of 2025, David Lynch died. In April, I attended a lovingly comprehensive film series at the Music Box Theatre in Chicago, where I spent over a full week watching films directed by Lynch or otherwise connected to him. Somehow, I didn’t anticipate how emotional of an experience it would be. It was contemplation of another type of empty space, the empty space a person creates when they die, an empty space full of grief, regret, confusion and mystery. That is a space, however, worth inhabiting. Lynch’s films are full of these spaces and they are also worth inhabiting. Not explaining or analyzing, as modern media pseudo-literacy strives to do at the expense of anything original or alive, but sitting in. Witnessing. Filling with ourselves. All these empty spaces full of all these opportunities for expansion. Lynch said that nothing really died, nothing was ever really lost, whether an idea or a person. It just changed form. From an empty space to a revelation and back again, remembered as a dream, constant in its absence, true in its subjectivity. A space just as significant, if not more so, than what it had contained. In the end, we should all be so lucky to be so witnessed.
